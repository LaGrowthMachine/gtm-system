# The narrative arc of a landing page

The arc is the buyer journey of the visitor, told as a story: who arrives, from where, what they already know, what
they fear, what moves them. The visitor is the hero. The offer is the guide that helps them avoid a threat. A page
converts when it tells the visitor's story, not the product's.

One arc per page (one visitor in one situation). The page, the ad that sends to it and the email that follows it all
draw from the same arc, so they speak to the same hero about the same threat.

## The 8 boxes

| # | Box | The question | Its role in the message |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hero** | Who exactly, in which situation? | Sets who we talk to and in which words |
| 2 | **Threat** | What do they risk losing if they do nothing? | Creates the stake |
| 3 | **What makes the threat real** | Which symptoms do they already see? | Makes the stake credible and immediate |
| 4 | **Rational problem** | What would they say in a meeting to justify a purchase? | Gives the avowable reason to act |
| 5 | **Unspoken emotional problem** | What will they never say out loud? | Gives the real reason to act. Guides the tone, never the text |
| 6 | **The guide** | Why this offer, in one credible sentence? | Positions the offer as the way out |
| 7 | **The proof** | What makes the promise credible for this hero? | Removes the doubt |
| 8 | **The transformation** | Who do they become after? What does their choice say about them? | Makes them want to act now |

A landing page uses all 8: hero section = entry angle + guide, problem = 3, solution = 4 → 6, proof = 7, call to
action = 8. The arc must cover the functional (box 4), the emotional (box 5) and the symbolic (box 8): if one is
missing, the page is flat.

### Quality test of each box

| Box | Where the material is | Quality test | Common trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Hero | CRM (roles and sizes that convert), personas | You can name 3 real people who match | "B2B companies": too wide to choose words |
| 2. Threat | Discovery calls, reasons in won deals | The loss is personal, not only business | An abstract threat ("lose efficiency") |
| 3. Threat made real | Call verbatims, support tickets, forums, reviews | Each symptom is observable this week | Symptoms invented by marketing |
| 4. Rational problem | Buying criteria in demos, comparisons asked for | The visitor could write it in an email to their boss | A list of features instead of a problem |
| 5. Unspoken emotion | What is said off the record, recurring objections, loss reasons | Different from box 4; never said in a meeting | Box 4 with an emotional adjective |
| 6. Guide | Positioning, what the offer does better than the alternatives | Passes the swap test: a competitor cannot sign the sentence | Starting with the product name |
| 7. Proof | Customer cases, product facts, logos, reviews | The proof comes from someone who looks like the hero | Prestigious logos with no link to the hero |
| 8. Transformation | Before and after reviews, what customers say of their role after | Covers the symbolic level: what the choice says about them | A functional benefit repeated ("save time") |

## Where each box's material comes from, by page type

| Box | Search, competitor query (conquest) | Search, generic query | AI-chat ads, retargeting, social | SEO page | Product, webinar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Visitor and situation | Search terms, SERP of the query | Keywords grouped by intent (% of volume), SERP | Contextual hints of the ad, site visitors | Queries of the page, SERP, "People also ask" | Who signs up, from which channel |
| 1. Hero | Personas, the competitor's target | Personas, the company size you serve | Personas | Personas | Personas, past attendees |
| 2. Threat | Price at scale, limits seen in the competitor's reviews | SERP ("Is X replacing Y?") | Previous tests, value propositions | SERP, forums | Sales notes |
| 3. Threat made real | **Competitor 1-3★ reviews**, counted by theme | "People also ask", forums (read by hand), your 4-5★ reviews ("before") | Your 4-5★ reviews, neighbours' reviews | Forums, PAA | Support tickets, webinar questions |
| 4. Rational problem | Value propositions, price comparison | Value propositions, PAA | Value propositions | The query itself | Value propositions |
| 5. Unspoken emotion | Almost always **Hypothesis**, with analytics hints | Hypothesis | Hypothesis | Hypothesis | Hypothesis, call notes |
| 6. Guide | "Their weakness, our strength" + swap test | Positioning | Positioning | Positioning | Positioning |
| 7. Proof | Sourced dated price, reviews of customers who left the competitor, a customer case | Ratings, customer count, logos with a result | Same | Same | Speaker, past results |
| 8. Transformation | Before and after reviews | Same | Same | Same | What attendees leave with |

## Filling rules

- **Boxes 3 and 5 in real words** (5 to 10 verbatims). Otherwise write "**Hypothesis.** Hints: ... **To source**:
  ...". Count the verbatims ("3 verbatims, to complete"). Note an absence as a fact.
- **Box 5 is always followed by** "**Never written**: guides the tone (calm and concrete, never "...")". The fear is
  answered by the tone, the visual and the proof. Written as is, it insults the visitor.
- **Box 7 always has a "Missing" line**: the ideal testimonial, the one that looks like the hero, that you do not
  have yet.
- **Competitor verbatims go in the arc, never on the page.** The page shows themes, counted honestly.
- **A capability is cited only if the user's product facts say so** (`landings/value-propositions.md`). A proof is a
  capability or a named customer, never an invented rate.
- **The offer is the guide, not the hero.** An arc that starts with "Our platform" is upside down.
- **Form follows substance.** A tone or a visual that contradicts the arc (playful on a "control" arc) cancels the
  message.

## Choosing the entry angle

The entry angle is the box that opens the page (the H1). Pick it from what the visitor already knows: the more aware
they are, the later in the arc you enter.

| Situation | Angle | Sequence | Example (invented) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Already compares (typed a competitor name, "alternative", "pricing") | **Proof + Rational** | 7 → 4 → 6 | "The Rivalboard alternative. Plans your team can read." |
| Knows the problem, compares categories of tools | **Rational**, the category named on the first screen | 4 → 6 → 7 | "Project tracking for teams that share people." |
| Comes from an ad (AI chat, social) | **The hero repeats the ad's promise word for word** | the ad's | Ad: "One board for every project" → H1 the same |
| Retargeting, knows the brand | Rational + transformation (Proof as a variant) | 4 → 8 | "Your plan, finally readable on Monday." |
| Cold audience, does not see the problem yet | **Threat** | 3 → 6 → 8 | "Three projects slipped last month. Nobody saw it coming." |
| Knows what they want, hesitates | **Transformation** | 8 → 7 → 6 | Final call to action, trial reminder |

The Rational angle is the control of any test. Threat grabs attention but wears out fast and attracts people outside
the target. Two arcs must not overlap: write "What sets it apart from the other arcs" (another visitor, another page).

## Testing an arc

1. Fill the whole arc and back boxes 3 and 5 with 5 to 10 real verbatims.
2. Write a hypothesis: "On [audience], opening with [angle] rather than [control angle] improves [metric]."
3. Make 2 or 3 variants that differ by the entry angle only. Only the hero changes.
4. Set the decision threshold before launch (conversions per variant, minimum duration). Do not stop early.
5. Read the result on the conversion, then on its quality. An angle that adds volume but lowers quality loses.
6. The winner becomes the control. Then test the next box: proof, then transformation, then visual.

## The order of the arc file

The user wants to understand the reasoning first, challenge it, then see the proposals and what they must decide.
The file `landings/<slug>/arc.md` follows this order:

1. **The reasoning**, in short prose: who types the query or sees the ad, what they find first (SERP, ad), what they
   lack (in their words), what we bring.
2. **The challenge**: table "what can make the page fail → what we do about it".
3. **The arc**: the 8-box table.
4. **The substance**: value propositions carried, proofs, claims checked (step 3 of the master).
5. **The proposals**: 2-3 H1 options, subline, pillars, hero visual, call to action, structure.
6. **To decide**: 3 to 5 numbered questions.
7. **Annexes**: queries × page alignment, SERP questions, variants by country.

Keep the file up to date when the page changes version (H1, structure). The history alone is not enough: the arc
table and the structure must reflect the live version.
